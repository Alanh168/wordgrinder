Maya pointed at the giant creature stirring in the park’s lake, as the other kids’ chanting flooded the air:
“Monster Maya! Monster Maya! Monster Maya!”
Maybe she was. If she could see things that others couldn’t, live in a world different than theirs, wasn’t she something alien? The thought slammed into her 6-year-old mind. 
Her parents jumped from the park benches when they saw the wild bob of ginger curls and teary, azure eyes running towards them. An exchange of knowing glances between the two. 
_It had happened again?_ 
_What terrible thing had she seen this time?_ 
They didn’t understand their daughter’s imagination but had hoped other kids would share it and partake. At least that’s what the therapist suggested. Anything to mitigate the development of antisocial behaviors.
Day turned to night. Windows painted Maya’s room with twilight’s moon as she lay on the floor, crayons scattered around her. She was supposed to be asleep by this time, but had been too afraid. Afraid, like many children, of monsters hidden in the closet or under the bed. But, for Maya, it was reality. Doodling was her means of escape.
However, hands grow tired.
Once hers ached enough, she finally ceased resisting and looked up. Her eyes traced the secrets of her room’s dark corners. They saw shapes, some static and others morphing, resembling things from this earth but just not…quite. Some loomed tall, others crept and slithered. Each had its own distinctive features. She was tired of being afraid. She gazed upon the things, looked at them for more than just a few seconds.
One was a limb with a cut, starting as bright red ooze, quickly drying and purpling, until it charred into black crusts. Whenever she looked away from it and back, the thing would revert to its original form and start the process again. It reminded her of the bruise she received at the playground that day.
Another: a cloud of darkness, startling her with a thunderclap whenever she tried looking away. Maya noticed it had first appeared when an earlier storm subsided.
Next: a cyclopean, reptilian eye. A crimson slit on a circular bed of bile, never ceasing to stare at her, no matter how many times she turned and looked back at it. It always stared. Maya couldn’t discern when this demon had appeared.
They, and many others, had been accompanying her everywhere and for so long. Soccer practice? Birthday parties? Even the bathroom? It didn’t matter. For 2 years they had been following her, on and off. And HOW they followed her. They seemed to get bigger from her fear, from her attention. So she tried depriving them of it, but to no avail. They were still there, creeping in and out of her life. She wanted, so desperately, to be free of them. 
Perhaps her avoidance infuriated them? She thought of this as her hand continued to ache from her escapist drawing. Her eyes and mind had grown weary from her prison of horrors. This was the first time she was just tired enough to face them unflinchingly.
And then a thought. If they wanted attention, she would give it to them. She pushed through the fear and the aching, and did the thing that she did best: draw. But this time with purpose. She would give the things a taste of their own visage.
She looked to and from the cut limb as her hands worked. Her final illustration: red ooze on the limb’s upper half, purple in the middle, and crusty black at the bottom. And when she was done, when she thought she had perfectly captured the thing, she took her drawing and presented it to the creature. But it was gone.
_What a butt_, the 6-year-old thought. She had worked hard on the piece and her intended audience just vanished? It wasn’t until the next time she tried drawing a creature that she realized what had actually happened. Maya didn’t understand it, but this was her first victory over the monsters.

Whispers. Indiscernible little rumblings with the occasional spike. Maya heard them crashing onto the gallery walls from the outside like ocean waves hitting some shore-bound structure. 
They came from the crowd of soon-to-be gallery perusers. Among them: family members, faculty, alumni, other students, and anyone who passed by an advertisement thinking it would be a fun way to spend their Saturday afternoon. 
“Hear that outside?” The Dean of the College of Arts exclaimed, “Among the crowd out there could be the means of your future!”
And that’s what this was really about, at least for those whose work was on display. Scouts from the country’s best graduate art programs came to these kinds of senior undergraduate exhibits. They came to asses talent, window shopping prospective students for their institutes and conservatories. Maya thought of their low acceptance rates. Never had teen numbers scared her so.
She scanned the room for the usual crop of star students, probing for signs of nervousness. Maya’s exhibit had been set up between those of her friends, the Barovich twins. To her right: Jade was coiling her dyed-green locks, her usual defense technique, making it indiscernible whether she was fidgety or flirting. To her left: Jack had his hands clasped behind his back, flannel-covered chest and goateed chin up, as if bracing for an incoming wave. Across the gallery, Isabelle scratched at her sun-spotted arm like she was digging it for gold. Harrison was in the gallery’s Northeast corner, doing the same scanning as Maya. His dark eyes met hers and she quickly looked away.
It had been since Highschool, three years ago, that Maya last identified as the “star artist.” North East Bard’s College collected such identities. This initially allured Maya but now made her feel small. She mentally, and sarcastically, thanked the Dean for reminding her of the pressure.
“If nothing comes of this, remember that you still have your final! Resilience is a necessary trait of any creator!”
Another pang of angst. Maya hadn’t started the project they were given three weeks ago.
The two sets of double doors on each side of the gallery burst open, a pressure finally released. A tide of whispers flooded the exhibition floor as attendees poured in from both sides. Maya could hear the _pitter-patter_ of their approach. She would be the last to receive them as she and the Barovich twins were in the middle of the gallery. The ocean of people and whispers became louder and louder until they were close.
They approached Jade’s wire sculptures first. Jade’s world was a world of aluminum: aluminum people, aluminum animals, and aluminum furniture. Maya admired how she could reconstruct life with such a base material, showcasing its skeletal elements without the maskings of color or texture. And the whispers poured over the aluminum skeletons, granting them flesh and life with their interpretations. These exhibitions were a level of vulnerability that seemed to make Jade uncomfortable, an artist’s skinny dip. She seemed to want to coil both sides of her hair, but restrained herself from doing so.
Then the whispers went for Jack. Jack’s world was one of beautiful utility. Bowls and pans and utensils and a chair. One would think he was moving into his section of the gallery. But the bowls had glass birds and ceramic flowers flowing from their brims. The chair was a velvety red V, a heart held together by lingerie-lace-like wiring. Jack’s exhibit was one where perusers were allowed, even welcomed, to touch. And so the whispers drowned Jack and his work.
And then the tides came for Maya. They started washing in slowly, meandering and stopping and then moving again. They came until they filled Maya’s little shop of horrors: her ink drawings and paintings of creepy disturbances.
There was the doctor, whose grin was devilish and all too excited. A flatline from an electrocardiogram served as the line that separated his upper, yellow-stained teeth from his lower, blackened ones. And he held a clipboard, turning it towards the viewer. But the “clipboard” was only a frame of a clipboard. Rather than pages, the clipboard frame held a mirror, cracked and marred with blood.
And there was the old cut limb. Maya drew it even when the limb beast wasn’t around. To her, it represented her power. Both because it was the first time she dispelled a monster, but also because of how far she had come. She drew the limb repeatedly because capturing the subtle flakes of skin-texture demanded practice. And her practice had paid off. Once drawn in simple crayon, the ink-lined limb now looked so real that she was afraid someone would mistake her for some trophy-collecting serial killer.
“Incredible! You are so talented.”
“I’m speechless right now.”
“I bet you could make insane Halloween decorations.”
Maya wondered if that last comment reflected a lack of “seriousness” people viewed her art with. Regardless, the few people that approached Maya were usually complimentary, their words like little islands of positivity. But it was the whispers, the ocean of potential criticisms, that soaked Maya’s mind. What invalidating thing would she find in its depths? 
Maya looked around the gallery. Perhaps the number of people staying in one location would be a sign of whose work was more “popular.”  Would that somehow denote who the scouts would be more interested in? Did the scouts pick students who drew more crowds of people?
Maya shook this off. She hated that her feelings about art had come to this:  measuring and scaling, comparing their value. She looked to her own work and remembered when it made her feel great to express her demons and show them to others. How other students gazed in awe. Maybe that was what she missed. How special she had once been.
Once the exhibition was over, curators started taking the students’ pieces back to storage for them. Maya’s doctor piece was being carried away as Harrison approached her. He took a passing glance at the piece and smirked.
“How freaky…” he grinned as he strode past her.
_Cocky cunt_, Maya thought.
Harrison’s world was the “real” world: a jumble of hair fibers, skin particles, wrinkles, sun spots, shadow gradients, and other mundane action captured in shouting, screaming sharpness. The dean made it painfully obvious that Harrison’s photo-realism was his favorite. The two walked alongside each other and out of the gallery like two love-struck teenagers.
“Looking forward to seeing that final you were telling me about,” Maya heard the dean say to him. And with that, the pressure was back on. Soon, students would hear from the schools whose scouts showed up. Maya could feel her heart racing.
Finals. Scouts. The rest of the day was supposed to be time to unwind. The students walked out together, relieving each other with stories of mutual nervousness. Maya joined them, but something kept her distracted. Despite the leave of gallery perusers, Maya could still hear the whispers.
She walked with her classmates out of the gallery, but she could catch their low rumble. The murmurs sat with her at the school’s lunch hall. Their airy echo followed her back to her dorm. And, when Maya was finally home, she waged her war against the monster of sound.
