
I find Grammy May lying naked with her stomach on the bed. Her legs and arms are bent and dig into the sheets, her body forming a smile. Paint freckles her pale forearms as they move shakily. She has to lift her head up and back in a slightly unnatural position just to see the canvas next to the bed. Two black streaks stem from the easel’s legs, over her white tiles, and out to the French double doors that connect her bedroom to the terrace. 

“Grammy,”  I gather myself. “What’s going on?”

“Change your perspective, change your art,” She says delicately, her attention fixated on steadying her arm. “I’m taking a break from a new darling:

\_<i>Indiana </i><i>Jones</i>…\_ 

“Indiana Jones and the Raiders of the Lost Ark” she confirms.

She painted her version of the movie poster yesterday and the day before yesterday and the day before that. Grammy’s career in Hollywood ended way before the Spielberg hit ever came out but she's never stopped painting her famous movie posters. Now she calls them “darlings," like she’s visiting lovers that got away.

++ Contest Exposition: Beginning ++

“Do you know what you’re going to submit for Round Two, Elizabeth?” She asks. It’s not meant as an accusation.

“I think so, Grammy” I whine.

“And Round Three?”

“Grammy, I’m not guaranteed to make it that far. If I lose two rounds of the competition, then I’m out.”

“That sounds terrible," she turns to me, "for the other students who are going to lose.” She pauses from her focus for a moment to flick some paint on my face. This brief second of surprise, I realize, is the first time my hand unclenches from a fist since I walked into Grammy’s room to check up on her. I noticed this naughty little habit a year ago when I started the MFA program. Now with the Bard Institute's student showcase coming, the hand has turned even more self-destructive. Grammy's paintings only remind me of the pressure. Whoever wins this competition makes it to the same annual showcase in Paris which launched her career.

++ Contest Exposition: End ++

Grammy’s world is a collection of memories from other worlds, carefully curated movie posters she painted in her glory days. On the wall behind her is <i>\_La </i><i>Dolce </i><i>Vita</i>\_, Marcello Mastroianni’s dark blue pearls staring at Anita Ekberg, looking generously bosomed upon the studio’s request to Grammy. Complimenting the pink of Anita Ekberg’s scarf is the reddish watercolor of Audrey Hepburn’s umbrella in \_<i>My </i><i>Fair </i><i>Lad</i>y\_ which hovers over the bed's headboard. Audrey stares at Ekberg as if in some private competition. Behind me, on Grammy's messy dresser is \_<i>13 </i><i>Ghost</i>s\_, its darkness seeming so out of place in a room with so much color. Grammy doesn't care, though, she was proud the studio chose her painting over famed horror poster artist Mel Hudson of <i>\_Little </i><i>Shop </i><i>of </i><i>Horrors</i>\_ fame. Ironically relegated to the dark corner next to Grammy’s closet is what most would consider the crown jewel of her career: \_Lawrence of Arabia\_.

“You put up \_Lawrence of Arabia\_ again? I thought you locked it away because Anthony Quinn was handsy when he was supposed to be modeling for you?”

“What?” Her voice is a shrill thing, contrasting the usual energy of her cadence. “I don’t remember that.” She carefully strokes the brush on her color palette. I can tell from her slowness that it’s giving her a bit of an Ab workout to paint in her position. “I-I-I remember Alex Guinness saying I reminded him of Rita Hayworth.” She stops painting for a moment “You didn’t answer me. What are you working on for Round 3?”I try to concoct some answer in front of Grammy’s Hollywood trophies but she reads me too quickly. “Oh honey, still stuck huh? Why-”

“Your memory gets me every time,” I say. “You remember the competition, Alex Guinness flirting with you, even my issues picking pieces for the competition, but you forget about your own clothes.”

Grammy puts brush and color palette down on the floor. The right side of her thin, wrinkled tummy raises itself from the bed. I throw my hand over my eyes to avoid seeing her private parts.

“Grammy!”

Her cackle ignites a giggle in me.

“Didn’t I pick out clothes for you to wear?” I ask her, hand still over my eyes.

“You did?”

I walk to her closet, open the folding plantation-style doors, and pull out a striped ruffle hem blouse and some jeans. “Here, put this on.”

It’s only now that I see what she’s been working on. Grammy's version of \_Indiana Jones and the Raiders of the Lost Ark\_ features the protagonist's being led by Indie down a dark corridor as the Nazi's look over them from above. Painted on Marion Ravenwood's body is the same outfit I laid out for Grammy earlier that morning.

“What? Grammy, you paint the clothes I lay out for you instead of putting them on?”

“Oh so you’re the one that showed me this. What an ugly outfit.”

“You’ve worn this for years” I say.

“Maybe that’s why it’s ugly now. The novelty’s gone.” She playfully flicks some paint on me from her brush. I feel my mouth contort into a smile. My parents are less amused by her antics but I’m always willing to keep eyes on her.

“Ok, you’re going to tell me what’s really wrong.” She says to me after I help her get dressed.

“I- I’ve got a lot on my mind, Grammy,” I say.

What do I tell her?

Do I say that I’ve just spent the last 6 hours ping ponging between a blank canvas and the breakup texts Dan sent me 2 weeks ago? Do I tell her how my walls are lined with the artwork I haven’t been able to sell? My little undesirables stare at me like I’m their mother hen, waiting for me to lead them somewhere. Do I tell her I’m the opposite of her as an artist. Her: accomplished and prolific. Me: unsellable and creatively crippled?

“I don’t know. I start on one piece, then start thinking about the work I currently have sitting on walls at a gallery. No one buys anything until the the gallery eventually tells me to take them and go. It’s been enough times now. I don’t want anymore.”

“Patience, honey” She says. Her light blue eyes are earnest but I can see her Hollywood trophies behind her. How patient did she have to be?

“When you paint, do you think about other things?” Grammy asks. The woman must be mystical in some way. That or she's sharper than most realize..

“Yes," my answer had Grammy May already getting up, "but maybe its that I stopped taking my Lexapro. It was working for my anxiety but kills my ability to generate ideas. I need to try oth-"

“Well, I have something for that.” She hands me the brush she’s been using, her “lucky brush.” Bristles wilted, green handle littered with specks of different paint colors. Its been with her for a few years now. We've all noticed her mood improve drastically since she started using it. Strange but a blessing.

“Grammy I can’t. You-”

“You can and you will. Paint whatever’s troubling you and watch it disappear. The damn little thing is better than any therapist.”

I look around her home: movie artwork seen by millions, tiny frames of family photos, signed memorabilia from her golden years. Why would such a life warrant therapy? 

\_<i>"You </i><i>always </i><i>do </i><i>this</i>"\_ Dan’s words ring in my head. His image is starting to come back to me now. The dumb innocent look his eyes always have. The little stubble I used to hate because it tickled my face when he kissed me. My stomach churns.

I feel a sudden tickle on my nose. I was staring off into space. Grammy’s now tickling my nose with the brush. My hand unclenches for another moment. Therapy indeed.
