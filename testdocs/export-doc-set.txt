The body is yellowed, waxen, jaw slack and open to the cold afternoon. From where I’m standing I have a direct shot up the untrimmed nostrils, a decent view of the halted blood, pooling and blue beneath the eyes. A high Wiccan priestess by the name of Cheetah has just unveiled the face, which she smudges with smoking bundles of sage. In sunglasses, a blue ballcap and a spotted dress worthy of her title, Cheetah implores the crowd to refer to the body exclusively as “the Traveler”—using his name will lure him back to the earthly plane and hinder his final journey to the land of Eversummer. She mutters a prayer and places the shroud back over the Traveler’s  face, leaving me to retie the knots tight against his cold, heavy skull.
They say you never forget your first. Technically, this isn’t mine—my first pagan burial, yes, and both the guidelines and the clientele are plenty foreign. But during my past year as a gravedigger, I’ve interacted with many bodies. I’ve hauled them from the funeral home gurney to the procession cart, I’ve lowered them inch-by-inch into the quartz-lined earth, I’ve shifted and rolled and tilted them to slide sheets out from beneath their heft to take home, throw in the rinse cycle, and reuse. But these bodies are hidden in some way—in the bureaucratic chain that follows death, cemeteries  are the last stop, and the bodies come to us pre-packaged, like the world’s weirdest Christmas. They are wrapped in linen shrouds, as the Traveler was, or encased in caskets of pine or willow wicker or simple cardboard. Over the past year, I’d seen only hints of bodies: the pallid hand poking through the shroud here, the liver-spotted foot there. But it wasn’t until Cheetah and her witchy ilk that I saw my first full, unfledged view of a dead human face—and this, as the kids say, hits different.
